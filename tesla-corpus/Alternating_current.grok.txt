[1] The magnitude of AC typically varies sinusoidally over time, producing a waveform that oscillates between positive and negative values. 
This periodic reversal enables AC to be efficiently transformed to higher or lower voltages using simple electromagnetic devices called transformers, making it ideal for long-distance power transmission with reduced energy losses compared to DC. [2] In the late 19th century , the practical implementation of AC systems revolutionized electricity distribution, culminating in the " War of the Currents " between proponents of DC, led by Thomas Edison , and AC advocates Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse . [3] Tesla's invention of the AC induction motor in 1887-1888 provided a reliable means to convert electrical energy into mechanical work without mechanical commutation, addressing key limitations of DC motors. [4] 
AC ultimately prevailed due to its superior scalability for powering cities and industries, as demonstrated by Westinghouse's successful electrification of the 1893 Chicago World's Fair and Niagara Falls in 1895 using AC transmission lines. [5] Today, AC dominates global electrical grids, operating at standard frequencies of 50 Hz in most countries or 60 Hz in North America , to supply residential appliances, industrial machinery, and large-scale infrastructure like railways and renewable energy systems . [6] While DC sees niche applications in electronics and batteries, AC's versatility supports nearly all modern power generation and grid transmission, with sources such as solar and wind often involving initial DC production converted to AC. Fundamentals 
Definition and basic principles 
Alternating current (AC) is an electric current in which the flow of electric charge periodically reverses direction, in contrast to direct current (DC), where the charge carriers flow unidirectionally. [9] This reversal occurs due to the periodic variation in the voltage applied across the circuit, causing the polarity of the source to switch repeatedly. [10] In practical applications, AC is most commonly generated in a sinusoidal waveform , where the current magnitude also varies smoothly between positive and negative peaks. [11] 
The key distinction from DC lies in the behavior of charge carriers, primarily electrons in conductors. In DC circuits, electrons experience a net drift in a single direction, resulting in steady energy transfer along the conductor. [2] With AC, the reversal of polarity causes electrons to oscillate or vibrate back and forth around a fixed position, with no net displacement over a complete cycle; however, this oscillatory motion still enables the propagation of energy through the circuit via electromagnetic fields. [12] This polarity inversion typically happens at a regular rate, defining the periodic nature of AC. 
A complete AC cycle consists of one full reversal from positive to negative and back, characterized by several fundamental parameters. The amplitude represents the maximum magnitude of the current or voltage during the cycle. [13] The period ( T T T ) is the duration required to complete one full cycle, measured in seconds. [14] The frequency ( f f f ) is the number of cycles per second, expressed in hertz (Hz), and is the reciprocal of the period: f = 1 / T f = 1/T f = 1/ T . [14] Additionally, the angular frequency ( ω \omega ω ), in radians per second, relates to the frequency by ω = 2 π f \omega = 2\pi f ω = 2 π f , providing a measure of the rate of oscillation in angular terms. [13] 
The concept of alternating current originated with the invention of the first practical AC generator by French instrument maker Hippolyte Pixii in 1832, based on Michael Faraday's principle of electromagnetic induction . [15] This device produced current that alternated direction as a permanent magnet rotated beneath a coil of wire. [16] 
Waveforms and cycles 
The primary waveform used in alternating current (AC) systems is the sinusoidal form, which describes the smooth, periodic variation of current or voltage over time. A cycle in an AC waveform refers to one complete sequence of variation, starting from zero, rising to the positive peak, returning to zero, descending to the negative peak, and returning to zero again, representing a full reversal of direction. [18] This cyclical behavior is fundamental to AC, with each cycle corresponding to one full rotation of the armature in a generator, linking the electrical period directly to the mechanical rotation speed. [18] The period T T T , or duration of one cycle, is given by T = 1 / f T = 1/f T = 1/ f , where f f f is the frequency ; for instance, at 60 Hz, T T T is approximately 16.67 milliseconds. [19] 
While sinusoidal waveforms are ideal for efficient power transmission due to their purity, real-world AC systems often encounter non-sinusoidal waveforms, such as square or triangular shapes, generated by nonlinear loads like rectifiers or switching devices. [20] These distorted waveforms contain harmonics— integer multiples of the fundamental frequency —that introduce higher-frequency components, leading to potential inefficiencies or interference in circuits. [20] A brief overview of Fourier series decomposition reveals that any periodic non-sinusoidal waveform can be represented as a sum of sinusoidal terms at the fundamental frequency and its harmonics, with coefficients determining the amplitude of each component, enabling analysis and mitigation of distortions. 
Visual representations of AC waveforms typically plot voltage or current against time on a graph, showing the sinusoidal curve oscillating symmetrically around the zero axis, with labeled axes for amplitude (peak value), time (period), and frequency markers for cycles; for non-sinusoidal cases, the plot would display sharper transitions, overlaid with harmonic sine waves to illustrate decomposition.

Generation and frequencies 
Common power frequencies 
The two predominant frequencies for alternating current (AC) power systems worldwide are 50 Hz and 60 Hz. The 50 Hz standard is used in most of Europe , Asia , Africa , Australia , and parts of South America , while 60 Hz is standard in North America , much of South America , and parts of Asia such as Japan and South Korea . [28] 
These frequencies originated in the late 19th century during the early commercialization of AC power . In 1891, engineers at Westinghouse Electric in Pittsburgh selected 60 Hz for its balance of efficiency in generators and reduced flicker in lighting systems, influenced by the design of alternating current generators driven by steam engines at typical speeds. That same year, engineers at the Allgemeine Elektricitäts-Gesellschaft (AEG) in Berlin chose 50 Hz, prioritizing lower transmission losses over long distances and compatibility with European generator designs, which set the standard for much of the world outside North America . [29] [30] 
Specialized applications employ other frequencies to meet specific operational needs. For instance, 16.7 Hz AC is used in railway electrification systems in Germany , Austria , and Switzerland at 15 kV, a legacy of early 20th-century single-phase AC traction technology that allowed efficient power delivery to locomotives without excessive transformer size. In aviation, 400 Hz AC is standard for onboard electrical systems in most civil and military aircraft , as the higher frequency enables smaller, lighter transformers and motors while maintaining power output, critical for weight-sensitive designs. [31] [32] 
Power grid operators maintain frequency stability within tight tolerances—typically ±0.05 Hz for 60 Hz systems and similar for 50 Hz—to ensure reliable operation. Deviations occur due to imbalances between generation and load; for example, excess demand can cause frequency to drop, while overgeneration leads to an increase. Such variations directly affect synchronous appliances like electric clocks, which derive timing from line frequency ; a sustained 0.1 Hz deviation over a day can cause a time error of about 144 seconds, potentially disrupting schedules or requiring manual adjustments. [33] [34]

AC generation methods 
Alternating current is primarily generated through electromagnetic induction , a process discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831, which states that a changing magnetic flux through a coil induces an electromotive force (EMF) according to Faraday's law : ε = − d Φ B d t \varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} ε = − d t d Φ B ​ ​ , where ε \varepsilon ε is the induced EMF and Φ B \Phi_B Φ B ​ is the magnetic flux . [35] This law forms the foundation for AC generation in rotating machines, where relative motion between a magnetic field and a conductor produces a periodically varying current, typically sinusoidal in form. [36] 
Synchronous generators, also known as alternators, are the most common devices for large-scale AC production, operating by rotating a rotor with permanent magnets or electromagnets within a stationary stator containing coils. [37] The rotor's constant speed, synchronized with the desired AC frequency, induces a sinusoidal voltage in the stator windings via electromagnetic induction , enabling efficient conversion of mechanical energy from sources like steam turbines or hydroelectric dams into electrical power. [38] These machines are designed for stable output, with the rotor field excited by direct current to maintain consistent magnetic flux during rotation. [37] 
Induction generators, a variant of asynchronous machines, are widely used in applications like wind power , where the rotor is driven above synchronous speed by variable mechanical input, such as turbine blades. [39] Unlike synchronous types, they require no separate excitation; instead, they draw reactive power from the grid to establish the rotating magnetic field , producing AC output that slips slightly in frequency relative to the rotor speed. [39] In wind turbines, squirrel-cage or doubly-fed induction generators (DFIGs) convert irregular wind energy into grid-compatible AC, with DFIGs allowing variable speed operation through partial power conversion in the rotor circuit for improved efficiency . [39] 
AC generation can be single-phase or polyphase, with three-phase systems preferred for balanced power delivery in industrial and utility applications due to their ability to provide constant power without the pulsations inherent in single-phase output. [40] In single-phase generators, a single coil or winding set produces one alternating waveform , suitable for smaller loads like household appliances. [41] Polyphase generators, such as three-phase alternators, use multiple offset windings to generate currents displaced by 120 degrees, resulting in smoother torque on the prime mover and more efficient transmission of power with reduced conductor size. [40] 
In modern renewable energy systems , inverters serve as a key method for AC generation by converting direct current from sources like solar photovoltaic panels into alternating current suitable for grid integration or local use. [42] These solid-state devices employ pulse-width modulation to switch DC input rapidly, synthesizing a sinusoidal AC waveform that matches grid standards, with efficiencies often exceeding 98% in contemporary designs. [42] In solar installations, inverters enable the aggregation of DC output from multiple panels into usable AC, supporting both grid-tied and off-grid configurations. [42] 
Transmission and distribution 
Long-distance power transmission 
Alternating current (AC) is the predominant form for long-distance power transmission due to its compatibility with transformers, which enable efficient voltage adjustment to minimize energy losses. At power generation sites, step-up transformers increase the voltage from typical generator outputs of around 13.8–25 kV to high levels such as 230 kV, 345 kV, 500 kV, or even 765 kV for transmission across the grid . [43] This elevation in voltage proportionally reduces the current for a given power level, as power P = V I P = V I P = V I , thereby decreasing resistive heating in the lines. The primary loss mechanism in transmission lines is ohmic heating, quantified by the formula P loss = I 2 R P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R P loss ​ = I 2 R , where I I I is the current and R R R is the line resistance; by lowering I I I , losses can be reduced quadratically, making high-voltage AC transmission far more efficient than low-voltage alternatives. In the United States, such systems result in approximately 5% of generated power being lost to heat and voltage drop during transmission and distribution (as of 2023). [44] 
The electrical grid's transmission infrastructure relies on key components to facilitate this high-voltage AC flow. Substations serve as critical nodes, housing step-up transformers near generators to boost voltage for bulk transfer and step-down transformers at intermediate or receiving ends to reduce it for further distribution; they also include circuit breakers, switches, and protective equipment to manage flow, isolate faults, and regulate voltage. [45] Transmission lines, which carry this power over hundreds or thousands of miles, are predominantly overhead, utilizing aluminum conductors on steel towers for cost-effectiveness—estimated at $2–3 million per mile for 345 kV lines (as of 2023)—while underground cables, often insulated with oil or gas for cooling, are reserved for urban or environmentally sensitive areas due to their fourfold higher installation costs and maintenance challenges. [46] [43] The U.S. grid features over 157,000 miles of high-voltage AC lines, forming interconnected networks that synchronize phases across regions for reliable delivery. [43] 
While AC dominates conventional transmission, high-voltage direct current (HVDC) offers advantages for ultra-long distances exceeding a few hundred miles, such as undersea or cross-continental links, where it achieves up to 50% lower losses by avoiding AC's reactive power and skin effects, using only two conductors instead of three. [47] However, AC's transformer-based voltage conversion remains simpler and less expensive for most applications, without needing costly converter stations required for HVDC. National grids typically employ three-phase AC systems, with phases offset by 120 degrees, to ensure balanced loads and efficient power delivery; this configuration requires 25% less conductor material than single-phase equivalents for the same power, enabling economical bulk transmission over vast distances. [48] For instance, three-phase lines in the U.S. interconnection support seamless power sharing across 642,000 miles of high-voltage infrastructure. [43] As of 2024, however, new high-voltage line construction has slowed significantly, with only 322 miles added—the third-lowest annual total in the past 15 years—posing challenges for integrating renewable energy sources and meeting growing demand. [49] 
Domestic and industrial supply 
In domestic settings, alternating current (AC) is typically supplied at standard nominal voltages tailored to regional electrical systems. In the United States , residential supply operates at 120 volts (V) for single-phase circuits, with a split-phase configuration providing 240 V for higher-load appliances like dryers and ovens, ensuring compatibility with common household devices. [50] In Europe , single-phase domestic supply is standardized at 230 V, supporting a wide range of lighting and small appliances across countries like Germany and France . [50] These voltages align with international norms defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) to promote safety and interoperability in low-voltage distribution. 
Wiring systems for homes emphasize single-phase AC delivery to minimize complexity and cost for everyday loads. Single-phase wiring uses two or three conductors—a hot wire, neutral, and often a ground —to distribute power from the service panel to outlets, powering outlets for lamps, refrigerators, and electronics . [51] Grounding is integral to these systems, connecting equipment enclosures to earth via a dedicated conductor to safely divert fault currents and prevent electric shock, as required by safety standards like UL 467 in the US . [52] Plugs and outlets follow region-specific designs for secure connections; for instance, US Type B plugs feature two flat pins and a round grounding pin, while European Type C or F outlets include protective earth contacts to comply with IEC and UL safety evaluations. [50] [53] 
Industrial applications rely on three-phase AC for efficient power delivery to heavy machinery, with standard voltages such as 400 V in Europe and 208–480 V in the US to handle balanced loads and reduce conductor sizes compared to single-phase equivalents. [54] Three-phase wiring employs four conductors—three phase wires and a neutral or ground—enabling smoother torque in motors and minimizing power fluctuations, which is essential for factories and workshops. AC motors , particularly induction and synchronous types, dominate industrial use; induction motors , the most common, power pumps and compressors through electromagnetic induction , offering robust, low-maintenance operation for continuous duties like water circulation in manufacturing. [55] Synchronous motors , which lock to the supply frequency for precise speed control, drive heavy machinery such as fans and grinders, enhancing energy efficiency in applications requiring constant velocity. [56] 
Frequency variations, such as 60 Hz in the US versus 50 Hz in Europe , can impact appliance compatibility; motors designed for 60 Hz may operate at reduced speed and higher temperatures on 50 Hz supplies, potentially leading to overheating or inefficiency without voltage adjustments. [57] Grounding practices in industrial three-phase systems further ensure safety by stabilizing voltages and mitigating fault risks, adhering to IEEE guidelines for commercial power grounding. [51] 
High-frequency effects 
Skin effect and proximity losses 
In alternating current (AC) systems, the skin effect refers to the tendency of the current to concentrate near the surface of a conductor rather than distributing uniformly across its cross-section, as occurs with direct current (DC). This phenomenon arises due to eddy currents induced by the time-varying magnetic field generated by the AC itself, which oppose the current flow in the conductor's interior and effectively confine it to a thin outer layer. [58] The skin effect was first theoretically derived by James Clerk Maxwell in 1873, highlighting how current density increases toward the conductor's surface in cylindrical geometries. [58] 
The characteristic measure of this effect is the skin depth, denoted as δ, which represents the depth below the surface at which the current density falls to approximately 37% (1/e) of its value at the surface. The skin depth is given by the formula: 
δ = 2 ω μ σ \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega \mu \sigma}} δ = ω μ σ 2 ​ ​ 
where ω is the angular frequency (ω = 2πf, with f being the frequency in Hz), μ is the magnetic permeability of the conductor (μ = μ₀ μᵣ, with μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m as the permeability of free space and μᵣ the relative permeability), and σ is the electrical conductivity of the material in S/m. [59] This concentration reduces the effective cross-sectional area available for current flow, thereby increasing the conductor's effective resistance compared to its DC value and leading to higher I²R losses. [60] 
Closely related is the proximity effect, which occurs when multiple conductors carrying AC currents are placed near each other, such as in transformer windings or bundled power cables. The alternating magnetic fields from adjacent conductors induce additional eddy currents in each other, causing further non-uniform current distribution and elevating the effective resistance beyond what the skin effect alone would produce. [60] This mutual influence distorts the current paths, concentrating them on the sides facing nearby conductors, particularly when currents flow in opposite directions, and results in increased power losses. [61] The proximity effect was analytically quantified by P. L. Dowell in 1966 , who developed equations for estimating these losses in layered windings, treating round conductors as equivalent foils to model the AC-to-DC resistance ratio. 
Both effects exhibit strong frequency dependence: the skin depth δ decreases inversely with the square root of frequency, making the phenomena negligible at power frequencies of 50 or 60 Hz for typical conductor sizes (e.g., δ ≈ 9–10 mm for copper at 50 Hz, exceeding the radius of most power line conductors), but increasingly significant above approximately 1 kHz where δ becomes comparable to or smaller than the conductor dimensions. [62] [59] In power transmission lines operating at 50/60 Hz, these effects contribute minimally to overall resistance, but they elevate I²R losses and cause additional heating in scenarios involving higher harmonics or elevated frequencies, potentially reducing transmission efficiency and requiring larger conductors to maintain capacity. [62] 
Practical examples illustrate their relevance beyond power grids. In audio frequency applications, such as speaker cables carrying signals up to 20 kHz, the skin effect can slightly increase resistance in thick solid-core wires, distorting low-level signals. At radio frequencies (RF) in antennas or transmission lines (e.g., above 1 MHz), the effects dominate, confining current to microns-deep layers in metals like copper , necessitating specialized designs like stranded or tubular conductors to minimize losses. [60]
